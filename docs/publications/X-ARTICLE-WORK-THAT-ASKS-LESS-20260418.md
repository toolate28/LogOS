# Work that asks less of you over time

Most of what you read about work getting better assumes it should also feel more demanding. Compound interest. The 10,000-hour rule. "What got you here won't get you there." The gospel is that if your practice isn't getting harder, you're not progressing — you've plateaued, and plateaus are bad.

But there's a separate pattern, less discussed, where the work genuinely improves and the thing you have to supply shrinks. The same paper that took you four hours last month takes ninety minutes this month, and the quality is better, and you can't point to any single trick that changed. The same client conversation that wrung you out two years ago now feels like a familiar river. The codebase that was a constant tax is, somehow, just running.

This is not plateauing. Plateauing is when quality stops rising while effort stays the same. What I'm describing is a different shape: quality rising, effort falling. Both curves bending toward each other.

It's worth having a name for it. I think it's a fixed point.

In math, a fixed point of a function *f* is a point *x* such that *f(x) = x*. You apply the function and nothing changes. If *f* is well-behaved — a contraction — then applying it repeatedly to any starting point pulls you closer and closer to the fixed point, and each pass moves you less than the last one. At the beginning you're a long way out, and every iteration is a big step. Near the end you're sitting almost still, and the function is barely moving you, because there isn't anywhere left to move to.

If you've been practicing something coherent for long enough, your activity is behaving like that contraction. Each pass of the work adjusts you — your taste, your technique, your defaults, your error-catching — and pulls you incrementally closer to some attractor: the version of you who does this competently. Near the beginning every pass is loud. Near the attractor every pass is quiet, and you'd be forgiven for thinking you weren't doing anything. But the quiet is the whole point.

What this means, practically, is that the experience of your work getting easier is ambiguous. Two very different things can feel identical from the inside.

One: you are converging. Your craft is stabilizing. You are running a well-conditioned iteration in a well-chosen function, and you've moved from early-iteration territory into late-iteration territory. The returns look smaller because the deltas are smaller, but the thing is actually better.

Two: you are regressing. Your craft is drifting. You've stopped paying attention and defaulted into the easy grooves. The thing is actually worse, and it feels easier because you're doing less of it.

These feel the same for a while. The difference is detectable only by the quality of the output, checked honestly against something external — your old work, someone else's good work, the world hitting it.

This is, I think, the most useful distinction I know for anyone in a long-haul creative or technical discipline. Not "have you plateaued" but "is your ease earned or borrowed." If the curve of quality is rising while the curve of effort is falling, you are inside a convergence you should protect. If quality is falling while effort falls, you are inside a decay you should interrupt.

Convergence is easy to undervalue because it doesn't perform the way compounding does. Compounding looks like exponential outward growth. Convergence looks like nothing. You can't screenshot a fixed point. Nobody retweets your sixth iteration of a function you've been running for five years. But the sixth iteration is where most of the value actually is.

There's a second pattern, layered on top, that I find even more useful. You can run fixed-point iterations inside fixed-point iterations. At some point you notice that *you* haven't just been converging inside a practice — your whole relationship to the practice has been converging toward a steady attractor, and the practice itself is just one of several things sitting in that larger basin. You return, a year or five later, to a problem you thought you'd solved, and realize you'd only sketched it at a coarser resolution. The same discovery at higher fidelity.

This feels uncannily like originality, and it often gets reported that way: *I had this new insight about X.* Sometimes that's genuine. But very often what you've done is take a return trip to a place you've already been, carrying better instruments than you had last time. The coordinates are familiar. The map is denser. The contour lines are closer together. You aren't in new territory. You're in old territory you're now able to see.

Recognizing a return trip as a return trip is an underrated move. The alternative — mistaking it for a first trip — forces you to rederive everything you already knew, while missing the real news, which is that your instruments got better and the same attractor is now legible to you in finer detail. You paid for those instruments; you should use them.

When you notice you're on a return trip, a few things change. You stop treating the discovery as fragile. You've been here before and it didn't vanish. You can afford to put scaffolding around it this time, because you know where the solid ground is. You can be less urgent. You have the rare latitude to work slowly and well on something you already trust, rather than frantically trying to nail something down before it gets away.

There's an accompanying risk, which is that you over-elaborate. You build scaffolding around a return-trip discovery that wants to stay lean. The cure is the same as for any craftsman: check your output against the raw artifact. Does the elaborated version actually do what the lean version did, better? Or have you just decorated? The fixed point remains the fixed point whether you write a book about it or a sentence.

The line I've most often wanted to give to someone early in a practice is something like: stay for the boring part. Don't leave when your work starts feeling uneventful. You're not stuck. You're inside a contraction. The drama of the first iterations is gone because there isn't as much distance left to travel. What the quiet period gives you isn't a thrill. It's a craft that works.

And the line I've most often wanted to give to someone mid-career is closer to: recognize when you're coming home. You don't have to pretend every new thought is new. Many of them are old thoughts in new instruments, and that is also valuable, and treating them as such is the adult posture. The alternative — forcing the novelty frame — leads to a discourse of perpetual discovery that discovers less and less. The other posture — patient recognition — leads to infrastructure. The difference is whether you write about the fixed point or build around it.

Most mature disciplines are a graveyard of fixed points surrounded by infrastructure people don't think about because it works. The infrastructure is the mark of return trips well taken. It's what you get when someone went to the attractor, came back, noticed they'd been there before, and decided to leave something solid for next time.

If your own work is getting quieter and you can't shake the feeling that it should be getting louder, check which curve is actually rising. If it's the quality one, the quiet is earned. Keep going. The next pass won't look like much from the outside either, and that's fine. The quiet is the signal that the iteration is near the attractor.

And if you find yourself back at a problem you thought you'd solved — don't panic, and don't perform re-discovery. You're on a return trip. Your instruments are better. Put something durable there. Then carry on.
